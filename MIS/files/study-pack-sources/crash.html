Block 1: Ch 1 - What an information system is

25 minutes. Then a 5-minute break.

1. Data vs information

In simple words: Data is raw facts. Alone they mean little. Information is data that has been processed so a person can use it to decide something.
Data (raw)Information (useful)
"331 Brite Dish Soap 1.29" - one beep at one supermarket checkout"Brite Dish Soap, Northwest region: 7,156 units sold, $9,231.24 this year"

A manager cannot act on one beep. A manager can act on the regional total: reorder stock, run a promotion.

Exam words: Data = streams of raw facts representing events. Information = data shaped into a form that is meaningful and useful to human beings.

2. An information system does 4 things

In simple words: An information system takes data in, works on it, gives useful results out, and uses the results to correct itself.
Exam words: An information system is a set of interrelated components that collect, process, store and distribute information to support decision making and control in an organization.
Memorize: Input (capture raw data) → Processing (classify, arrange, calculate) → Output (send information to the people who need it) + Feedback (output sent back to check or correct the input).

Example: UPS package tracking (this is Case 1, Q1)

InputProcessingOutput
Bar-code label scanned at every stop. The driver's handheld DIAD records the signature and delivery.Central computers update each package's status. ORION (AI) works out each driver's best route.Tracking page for customers. Proof of delivery. Daily route for each driver. Reports for managers.
Trap: A computer is not an information system. The computer only stores and processes. The information system also needs people, procedures, data and the organization around it.

3. Three dimensions: Organization, Management, Technology

In simple words: Technology alone does nothing. People inside an organization must use it, and managers must decide what to do with it. So every information system has three sides.
DimensionWhat it meansUPS example
OrganizationThe people, the levels (senior, middle, operational), the departments (sales, HR, finance, production) and the cultureDrivers, dispatchers; culture of "best service, lowest rates"
ManagementManagers see problems, make decisions and make action plansManagers decided to use AI routing to cut fuel and miles
TechnologyHardware, software, data management, networks and telecommunicationsDIAD, bar codes, central computers, mobile network, website and app
Memorize: O-M-T. Use it as your answer frame. Whenever a question says "management, organization and technology factors", write three short paragraphs: Organization, Management, Technology.
Complementary assets, in simple words: Buying a system is like buying a gym membership. It only gives value if you also change your habits. Firms must also change processes, train people and get management support.
Exam words: Complementary assets are the assets required to derive value from a primary investment (new business processes, training, management support, a supportive culture). This is why two firms that buy the same system get different results.

4. Six business objectives: why firms spend on information systems

Memorize (6), in this order: Operational excellence, New products/services/business models, Customer and supplier intimacy, Improved decision making, Competitive advantage, Survival.
Hook: "Our New Customers Increase Company Survival".
ObjectiveIn simple wordsExample
1. Operational excellenceDo the same work faster and cheaperWalmart; UPS ORION saves about 100 million miles a year
2. New products, services, business modelsIS makes new things to sell, or new ways to earnApple iPhone + iTunes; Netflix
3. Customer and supplier intimacyKnow customers and suppliers well, so they stayLuxury hotels remember each guest's preferences
4. Improved decision makingDecide with real-time data, not guessesVerizon's real-time web dashboard
5. Competitive advantageDo things better than rivals: lower price, better quality, fasterApple, Walmart, UPS
6. SurvivalYou must have it to stay in business: the industry or the law forces itCitibank's ATMs forced other banks to install ATMs
Trap: survival vs competitive advantage. Everyone already has it and you need it just to stay alive = survival. It puts you ahead of rivals = competitive advantage.
Self-check: Block 1
  1. "Branch 3 total sales in October: Tk 12 lakh." Data or information? Why?
  2. A UPS driver's DIAD records a customer's signature. Which of the 4 activities is this?
  3. A bank installs a mobile app only because every other bank already has one. Which objective?
Answers: 1. Information: it is processed and summarized so a manager can use it. 2. Input. 3. Survival.

Block 2: Ch 2 - Business processes and the four system types

25 minutes. This is the most important part of Ch 2.

1. Business process

In simple words: A business process is the set of steps a company follows to get one job done, for example taking an order and delivering it.
Exam words: A business process is the collection of activities required to produce a product or service, together with the way the firm coordinates work, information and knowledge.
  • Order fulfillment is cross-functional: sales takes the order, accounting checks credit and bills, production or the warehouse ships it. One process, three departments.
  • IT improves processes in two ways: it makes old steps faster (automatic credit checks, e-invoices), or it creates new processes (streaming music instead of selling CDs; sending money with bKash instead of going to a bank).

2. The four system types: one company story

In simple words: Different managers need different information. Staff at the bottom record what happens. Middle managers check performance and test options. Top managers look at the future and the outside world.

Story: a supermarket chain like Shwapno.

  • TPS: every checkout records each sale and updates stock. It does the same routine job every time.
  • MIS: each month the regional manager gets a report: sales of each branch vs target. It summarizes TPS data in a fixed format.
  • DSS: the manager asks, "If we cut the price of rice by 5%, how much will profit change?" It runs a model on the data: a what-if question.
  • ESS: the CEO's dashboard shows company totals plus outside data (competitors, inflation) to decide "Should we open 20 new branches?"
SystemWho uses itInputOutputDecision type
TPS Transaction processingOperational staff and managersTransactions (sales, timecards)Detailed reports, lists, payslipsRoutine, structured, rules decided in advance
MIS Management informationMiddle managersSummarized TPS dataSummary and exception reportsRoutine questions decided in advance
DSS Decision supportMiddle managers, analystsInternal + external data + analytical modelsInteractive "what-if" analysisNon-routine, unique, fast-changing
ESS Executive supportSenior managersSummarized internal data + external events (tax laws, competitors)Dashboard (portal), projectionsNon-routine; needs judgment and insight
Memorize: TPS records. MIS reports. DSS asks what-if. ESS looks outside.
Levels: TPS = bottom (operational). MIS and DSS = middle. ESS = top (senior).
Trap 1, MIS vs DSS: MIS answers questions decided in advance (the same monthly report). DSS answers a new question using a model.
Trap 2, DSS vs ESS: ESS is for top managers and brings in external data.
  • TPS is the main source of data for all the other systems. If the TPS fails, the business stops.
  • MIS, DSS and ESS are called business intelligence systems: tools that organize and analyze data so managers decide better.

3. Enterprise applications: whole-company systems

In simple words: Instead of each department having its own separate system, one system links the whole company, or links the company with outside partners.
ApplicationWhat it doesWhy
ERP (enterprise system)One software system and one central database for all departmentsNo duplicated or conflicting data
SCM supply chain managementLinks the firm with suppliers and logistics firms: orders, stock, deliveryRight amount, shortest time, lowest cost
CRM customer relationship managementEverything about customers: sales, marketing, serviceKeep customers and sell more to them
KMS knowledge managementCaptures and shares employees' knowledgeExpert know-how is not lost
Hook: SCM = Suppliers. CRM = Customers. KMS = Knowledge. ERP = Everyone inside, one database.
TermMeaning
IntranetInternal company website, employees only
ExtranetCompany website also open to authorized suppliers and vendors
E-businessRunning the firm's major business processes with digital technology and the Internet
E-commerceBuying and selling online (Daraz). A part of e-business.
E-governmentGovernment serving citizens and businesses online (NID, passport, tax)
Self-check: Block 2
  1. A Pathao manager tests "What if we raise fares at 8 pm?" with a model. Which system?
  2. Which system is the main data source for all the others, and why?
  3. Which enterprise application links a firm with its suppliers?
Answers: 1. DSS (a new question, needs a model). 2. TPS, because it records every transaction the others summarize. 3. SCM.

Block 3: Ch 2 - Collaboration, knowledge, IS jobs, Case 2

20 minutes.

1. Collaboration

In simple words: People working together toward the same clear goal.
Exam words: Collaboration is working with others to achieve shared and explicit goals. A team is a formal group with a specific mission.
Benefits (5): Productivity, Quality, Innovation, Customer service, Financial performance.
Hook: "Please Quit Ignoring Customer Feedback".
  • Tools alone are not enough. Good collaboration needs collaboration capability (open culture, decentralized structure) plus collaboration technology. Buying Microsoft Teams changes nothing if the boss decides everything alone.
  • Social business = using social networks to engage employees, customers and suppliers. Key word: conversations.

2. Time/space matrix: which tool fits

In simple words: Ask two questions. Are we working at the same time? Are we in the same place?
Same placeDifferent place
Same timeFace-to-face meeting, interactive whiteboardVideo call (Zoom, Teams), instant messaging
Different timeTeam rooms, shared displays, shift workEmail, wikis, shared Google Doc, group calendars

3. Knowledge

  • Tacit knowledge = in people's heads, never written down (how a senior engineer knows a machine is about to fail).
  • Explicit knowledge = written down (manuals, reports).
  • Three types of KMS: enterprise-wide KMS, knowledge work systems, intelligent techniques.

5. Case 2: Do virtual meetings help collaboration?

  • Facts to quote: "Zoom fatigue" (tiredness and burnout from constant video calls; UNC Charlotte survey of 150 employees). Carnegie Mellon study: audio-only teams did better than video teams. Meta, Slack and Asana use no-meeting days.
  • They help: work from anywhere, real-time meetings across locations, less travel, screen sharing and recordings.

They hurt: answer with O-M-T

FactorProblem
ManagementToo many meetings; meetings in the most productive hours; cameras used to watch staff
OrganizationCulture of always being online; no informal contact; remote staff feel left out
TechnologyVideo pulls attention from voice cues; always-on camera and self-view are tiring; staff with weak tech skills get excluded

Fixes: fewer, shorter meetings with an agenda; work asynchronously (shared doc instead of a meeting); breaks; a moderator so everyone speaks; cameras optional, self-view off; meeting-free days.

Block 4: Ch 3 - Porter's five forces

25 minutes. The teacher's slide 7 says: "Please remember this, because I won't provide them with the question."

In simple words: Think of an industry's profit as one pizza. Five forces decide how much of it the firms in the industry keep. The stronger the forces, the smaller the slice, so the less profit.
Exam words: Porter's competitive forces model is a framework for analyzing a firm's competitive environment. The intensity of the five forces determines how profitable the industry is.
Threat of new entrants
Bargaining power of suppliersRivalry among existing competitors (the firm)Bargaining power of customers
Threat of substitute products or services

The five forces one by one

ForceWhy it hurts profitStrong whenHow IS fights it
1. Threat of new entrantsHigh profits attract new firms. They sell cheaper to win customers, so everyone's prices and margins fall.Margins are high and barriers to entry are lowA system that is expensive or hard to copy becomes a barrier to entry (UPS's tracking network)
2. Bargaining power of suppliersPowerful suppliers raise prices or set terms, so your costs rise.Few suppliers, strong brand, no substitute input, you buy only a littleOnline buying from many suppliers; SCM
3. Bargaining power of customersPowerful buyers push prices down and demand more quality.Few big buyers, easy to switch, prices easy to compare onlineCRM and loyalty programs raise switching costs
4. Threat of substitutesA different product meets the same need, so you cannot raise prices.Many substitutes, low switching costsSwitching costs; unique products
5. Rivalry among existing competitors (centre)Firms in the same industry fight with price cuts, advertising and R&D, so margins fall.Many similar firms, low entry barriers, many substitutesUse IS for one of the four strategies (Block 5)
Exam wording (write these from memory):
  1. New entrants: Industries with large profit margins attract new firms, which sell at lower prices to win market share. This pushes margins down. The threat depends on the strength of barriers to entry.
  2. Suppliers: Suppliers of raw materials, components, labor and services (the market of inputs) can raise prices or set terms when they are few, powerful or have no substitutes.
  3. Customers: Customers (the market of outputs) can push prices down and demand better quality and terms, especially when switching is easy.
  4. Substitutes: A substitute uses a different technology to meet the same need. More substitutes push prices and margins down.
  5. Rivalry: Rivalry among existing competitors is the main determinant of industry competitiveness, so it sits in the centre. Fierce rivalry cuts margins through price wars, advertising and R&D.
Trap: rival vs substitute vs new entrant.
Rival = same product, another firm already in the industry (Starbucks vs another coffee shop).
Substitute = different product, same need (coffee vs tea or juice).
New entrant = a firm not in the industry yet, about to join.

Block 5: Ch 3 - Strategies, Internet, value chain, value web

25 minutes.

1. The four generic strategies

In simple words: The five forces show the danger. The four strategies are the four ways to win anyway. Information systems help with each one.
StrategyIdeaHow IS helpsExample
Low-cost leadershipBe the cheapest while keeping quality and serviceCut waste: automatic restocking, efficient logisticsWalmart, Asda, easyJet
Product differentiationOffer something unique that others cannot easily copyCreate new products or a better customer experienceApple iPhone, Google
Focus on market nicheServe one narrow market better than anyoneData on that customer group to target it wellHilton Hotels; Amazon Prime
Customer and supplier intimacyBuild strong ties so customers and suppliers stayCRM, SCM, loyalty programs raise switching costsTesco, Amazon, DHL
Memorize (4) in plain words: Cheapest - Different - Narrow - Close.
= Low-cost leadership, Product differentiation, Focus on market niche, Customer and supplier intimacy.

How strategies answer forces (from the group discussion note): a cost leader can survive price wars and powerful buyers; differentiation makes buyers loyal, so they have less power and ignore substitutes; a niche firm meets needs rivals cannot; intimacy raises switching costs, so buyers do not leave.

2. The Internet's effect on the forces

  • Lower barriers to entry, so more new entrants. More substitutes.
  • Customers see all prices online, so buyer power goes up.
  • Rivalry is more intense: wider market, more competitors, fewer differences between them.
  • Some industries were transformed or destroyed: travel agencies, printed encyclopedias, newspapers.
  • The good side: new ways to build a brand and loyal customers online.

3. Value chain

In simple words: See the firm as a chain of steps. Each step adds value to the product. Put information systems where they add the most value.
Exam words: The value chain model sees the firm as a series of activities that add value to its products. It shows where competitive strategies can best be applied and where information systems have the most strategic impact.
Primary activities (make and deliver the product)Support activities (make the primary ones possible)
1. Inbound logistics: receive and store materials
2. Operations: turn inputs into products
3. Outbound logistics: store and ship products
4. Sales and marketing
5. Service: after-sales support		 1. Organization infrastructure: administration, management
2. Human resources
3. Technology: improve products and processes
4. Procurement: buying inputs
Remember primary by following the product: it comes in → is made → goes out → is sold → is serviced.
Support: the office, the people, the tech, the buying.

Example, a garment factory: receiving fabric (inbound) → sewing (operations) → shipping to the buyer (outbound) → winning orders (sales) → handling returns (service). Customers decide how much value each step adds.

4. Value web

In simple words: Instead of one firm doing every step, many independent firms link up with IT and serve the customer together.
Exam words: A value web is a collection of independent firms that use IT to coordinate their value chains to produce a product or service for a market collectively. It is more customer-driven and less linear than a value chain.
Value chainValue web
ScopeOne firmMany independent firms linked by IT
ShapeStraight line of stepsFlexible network, driven by customers

5. Four quick terms (MCQ)

  • Synergies: units or firms share outputs, markets and expertise, so costs fall and profits rise (JP Morgan Chase's bank mergers).
  • Core competency: an activity where a firm is world-class.
  • Network economics: each extra user adds value at almost no extra cost (Facebook, eBay).
  • Scalability: the business can grow sales without costs rising in proportion (apps, online stores, McDonald's franchising).
Self-check: Block 5
  1. Name the four generic strategies.
  2. List the five primary activities in order.
  3. Give one difference between a value chain and a value web.
Answers: 1. Low-cost leadership, product differentiation, focus on market niche, customer and supplier intimacy. 2. Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, sales and marketing, service. 3. A value chain is one firm's activities; a value web links many independent firms with IT to serve a market together.

Block 6: Exam practice - how to write answers

30 minutes.

What the exam rewards (course outline): 50 marks. Remember 5, Understand 15, Apply 15, Analyze 10, Evaluate 5. Only 5 marks are pure memory. The other 45 come from explaining an idea and using it on a company. This block shows how.

1. The 4-step answer for any written question

P-E-E-E: Point (name the model or term) → Explain (one sentence of exam words) → Example (use the company in the question) → Effect (so what: profit, cost, customers).

Example question: "How does a DSS help a Pathao manager?"
Point: A DSS is a decision support system. Explain: it supports non-routine decisions by combining data with analytical models for "what-if" analysis. Example: a Pathao manager can test what happens to rides and income if fares rise 10% in rush hour. Effect: better pricing decisions and higher profit without guessing.

2. Five forces question: the frame

  1. One opening line: "Porter's competitive forces model analyzes the competitive environment; the intensity of the five forces determines industry profitability."
  2. For each force: rating (high / medium / low) + one reason with a fact + one IS response.
  3. Conclusion: is the industry attractive? Which of the four strategies should the firm use?

3. Model answer: the airline industry (your group discussion)

ForceRatingReason
RivalryHighDelta, United, American plus low-cost Southwest, JetBlue, Spirit. Fares are about the same everywhere, so no firm earns extra profit. High exit barriers keep firms fighting.
New entrantsLow to mediumHuge capital, operating costs and complex regulation. No airline started in this century has even 2% market share.
SuppliersHighThree main inputs: fuel (world oil price), aircraft (only Airbus and Boeing), labor (strong unions).
CustomersHighComparison websites show every fare; switching costs are near zero.
SubstitutesMediumRoad, rail and water compete on short routes; weak on long international routes where time matters.

Conclusion: Most forces are strong, so airline profits are low. Airlines use low-cost leadership (Southwest, easyJet) or customer intimacy (Delta's loyalty rewards) to survive.

4. Case question frame

1. State the problem in one line. 2. Name the model (O-M-T, five forces, system types, objectives). 3. Use 2-3 facts from the case. 4. End with a recommendation.

Answers: 1. Point, Explain, Example, Effect. 2. Rating, one reason with a fact, one IS response. 3. Few powerful suppliers: fuel, aircraft (only Airbus and Boeing), labor (unions).
