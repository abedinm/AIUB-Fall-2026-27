  • Firms spent more than $20 trillion on IT worldwide 2019-2023; another $5 trillion expected in 2024 (Gartner).
  • About 2.9 billion people expected to buy something online in 2024.
  • 92% of all jobs and 95% of entry-level jobs need digital skills (Figure 1.1).
  • Technology changes how you work, where you work, and how well you are paid.

The digital firm

1.3 What an information system is

  • Information technology (IT): the hardware and software a business uses to achieve its objectives.
  • Information system (IS): interrelated components that collect (retrieve), process, store and distribute information to support decision making and control; also help with analysis, visualization and product creation.
  • Data = streams of raw facts (e.g. "331 Brite Dish Soap 1.29" from each checkout).
  • Information = data shaped into a meaningful, useful form (e.g. "Brite Dish Soap, Northwest region, 7,156 units sold, YTD sales $9,231.24").
Four activities (Figure 1.4): Input (capture raw data) → Processing (classify, arrange, calculate) → Output (send information to people/activities) + Feedback (output returned to correct or evaluate input).
The IS sits inside the organization, which sits in an environment: customers, suppliers, competitors, stockholders, regulatory agencies.
  • A computer or a program is not an information system. Computers are only the storage and processing part.
  • MIS (the field) studies both technical and behavioral issues around developing, using and the impact of IS in firms.

1.5 Complementary assets

  • Complementary assets = assets required to derive value from a primary investment.
  • Buying technology alone does not create value. The firm also needs the right organization and management around it.
  • (textbook) Examples: new business processes and workflow, training, management support, a culture that values efficiency, good infrastructure.
  • The business perspective stresses the organizational and managerial nature of IS.

Case 1: UPS Competes Globally with Information Technology

Source: MIS18_Case_1.pdf (textbook pp. 18-19).

Case facts to quote

  • Started 1907; ~25 million packages a day, 220+ countries; 500,000+ employees, 125,000+ vehicles, 557 aircraft.
  • Spends about $1 billion a year on IT. Strategy: "best service and lowest rates".
  • 743.5 million tracking requests per day.
  • Competitors: FedEx, US Postal Service, Amazon.

Q1. Inputs, processing and outputs of the package tracking system

InputsProcessingOutputs

Q2. Technologies used and the role of AI

  • Scannable bar-code labels; handheld DIAD (Delivery Information Acquisition Device) on a cell network.
  • Wired and wireless networks, desktop computers, central computers and storage, tracking software, website and mobile app.
  • DeliveryDefense (AI): predicts the chance of a successful delivery; flags addresses likely to have "porch pirates"; reroutes at-risk parcels.
  • Network Planning Tools (AI): reroute parcels around storms and trouble spots; machine learning handles huge data volumes.
  • ORION (AI routing): best route per driver from traffic, weather, stops. Saves ~100 million miles and 10 million gallons of fuel a year.
  • Role of AI: prediction and optimization - fewer losses, faster routes, lower cost.

Q3. Business objectives addressed

  • Operational excellence: automated tracking and ORION routes cut miles, fuel and cost.
  • Customer intimacy: customers track, print labels and reroute parcels themselves.
  • Improved decision making: real-time data for dispatchers and managers; NPT reroutes around storms.
  • New products/services: DeliveryDefense offered to customers (e.g. Texas Precious Metals).
  • Competitive advantage: low cost + high service keeps UPS ahead of FedEx, USPS and Amazon.

Q4. What if the systems were unavailable?

  • No tracking: customers and service reps could not find any of 25 million daily packages.
  • Drivers would lose routes: more miles, more fuel, late deliveries.
  • More lost and stolen parcels; no proof of delivery.
  • Customers would switch to FedEx or Amazon; UPS would lose its competitive advantage.
  • At UPS's scale the business would effectively stop (IS = survival).

2.1 Business processes

  • Business process = the collection of activities required to produce a product or service, plus the unique way a firm coordinates work, information and knowledge.
  • A business can be seen as a collection of business processes.
  • Processes can be assets (well designed = competitive strength) or liabilities (inefficient = slow, rigid).
  • Can belong to one function or be cross-functional.
FunctionExample processes (Table 2.1)
Manufacturing and productionAssembling the product, checking quality, producing bills of materials
Sales and marketingIdentifying customers, making them aware, selling
Finance and accountingPaying creditors, creating financial statements, managing cash
Human resourcesHiring, evaluating job performance, enrolling in benefits
  • Order fulfillment (Figure 2.1) is cross-functional: sales, accounting and manufacturing must coordinate. A Daraz order crosses seller, payment provider, warehouse and rider.
  • IT improves processes in two ways: (1) more efficient old processes (automate credit checks, invoices); (2) entirely new processes (Amazon books, Apple Music streaming, bKash money transfer) - change information flow, run steps in parallel, remove decision delays.

2.4 Collaboration and social business

  • Collaboration = working with others to achieve shared and explicit goals. Short or long, one-to-one or many-to-many.
  • Team = formal group with a specific mission (e.g. "increase online sales by 10%").
  • Why more important today (6): changing nature of work; growth of professional work; changing organization of the firm; changing scope of the firm (global); emphasis on innovation; changing culture of work.
  • Social business = using social networks (Facebook, X, internal tools) to engage employees, customers and suppliers. Key word: conversations. Needs information transparency.
Benefits (Table 2.3, memorize 5): Productivity, Quality, Innovation, Customer service, Financial performance.
  • Figure 2.7: collaboration quality = collaboration capability (open culture, decentralized structure) + collaboration technology → better firm performance. Buying a tool without the culture changes nothing.

Tools

  • Email, IM, Slack (channels, file sharing, calls).
  • Virtual meetings: Teams, Zoom, Chime, FaceTime. Telepresence = appearing present somewhere you are not.
  • Cloud collaboration: Google Drive/Docs/Workspace, OneDrive, Microsoft 365. Teams = conversations; SharePoint = documents and content.
  • Human-AI collaboration: humans and AI combine complementary strengths toward shared goals (Toyota AgentAsk).
  • Enterprise social networking: Salesforce Chatter, Microsoft Viva Engage (profiles, updates, permissions and privacy).
  • Virtual worlds / metaverse: 3D spaces with avatars.
Time/space matrix (Figure 2.8)Same place (colocated)Different place (remote)
Same time (synchronous)Face-to-face meetings, interactive whiteboardsVideo/web conferencing, IM

Six steps to choose a tool: (1) locate the firm in the time/space matrix; (2) list products for each problem cell; (3) cost-benefit incl. training; (4) security risks; (5) ask users about implementation and training; (6) pick candidates, invite vendor presentations.

Knowledge management

  • Tacit knowledge = in people's heads, not documented. Explicit = documented. 80%+ of business content is unstructured or semi-structured.
  • Three KMS types (Figure 2.9): enterprise-wide KMS, knowledge work systems, intelligent techniques.
  • Enterprise-wide KMS: ECM (enterprise content management, needs a taxonomy = classification scheme), wikis, expert directories, LMS (learning management system, e.g. Google Classroom, Moodle).

Case 2: How Much Do Virtual Meetings Help Collaboration?

Source: MIS18_Case_2.pdf (Ch 2 case, textbook p. 64).

Case facts to quote

  • ~13% of full-time US employees still work from home; ~28% hybrid.
  • Before Covid, 70%+ of managers thought meetings were costly and unproductive.
  • "Zoom fatigue": tiredness, anxiety, burnout from constant video calls (UNC Charlotte survey of 150 employees).
  • Carnegie Mellon (Anita Williams Woolley): audio-only teams scored higher than video teams on a joint task.
  • No-meeting days: Meta, Clorox, Slack, Asana, Twilio.

Q1. How do virtual meetings enhance collaboration?

  • Employees work and collaborate from home or in hybrid models.
  • Managers can manage teams from far away.
  • Real-time meetings across locations; less travel cost.
  • Screen sharing, chat, recordings, shared files support teamwork.

Q2. How do they hinder collaboration? (management, organization, technology factors)

FactorProblem
ManagementToo many meetings; meetings during most productive hours; weak facilitation; managers use video to monitor (surveillance)
OrganizationCulture of constant connectedness; remote/hybrid work removes informal social contact; isolation widens the manager-employee gap; "in" and "out" groups
TechnologyVideo pulls attention from audio cues; fewer social cues; self-view and always-on cameras are tiring; staff with weak tech skills get excluded

Result: fatigue, isolation, exclusion, self-censorship, lower-quality collaboration.

Q3. How can organizations improve collaboration technology use?

  • Fewer, shorter meetings with clear agendas.
  • Asynchronous work: e.g. a shared Google Doc instead of a meeting.
  • Breaks in long meetings and between back-to-back meetings.
  • Active moderation so everyone gets to speak.
  • Turn off self-view; make cameras optional.
  • Meeting-free time blocks or days.

Q4. A 500-employee company wants virtual meetings. Issues to consider

  • Needs: which work must be same-time vs different-time (time/space matrix).
  • Tool choice by the six steps: cost incl. licences and training, security and privacy risks, user input, vendor demos.
  • Fit with existing tools (e.g. Microsoft 365) and network bandwidth/devices for remote staff.
  • Meeting policies: limits, camera rules, no-meeting days; employee well-being and fatigue.
  • Inclusion of less tech-skilled staff; avoid using video for surveillance.
Self-check Case 2
  1. What is "Zoom fatigue"?
  2. Which teams did better in the Carnegie Mellon study, and why?
  3. Give three fixes the case recommends.
Answers: 1. Tiredness, anxiety or burnout from constant videoconferencing. 2. Audio-only teams; video draws attention away from audio cues and turn-taking. 3. Any three: asynchronous work (shared doc), breaks, active moderation, self-view off / cameras optional, meeting-free days.

3.1 Porter's competitive forces model

A framework for analyzing a company's competitive environment. The number and power of rivals, new entrants, suppliers, customers and substitutes decide how profitable the industry is.

Threat of new entrants
Bargaining power of suppliersRivalry among existing competitors
(the firm)		Bargaining power of customers
Threat of substitute products or services
Exam-ready wording: write these from memory.
ForceExam explanationHigh when...
1. Threat of new entrantsIndustries with large profit margins attract new firms. To win market share, entrants sell at lower prices, so existing firms must cut prices too. This puts downward pressure on profit margins. The force depends on the strength of barriers to entry. IT that is difficult or expensive to copy creates a barrier and lowers the threat.Margins are high and barriers to entry are low
2. Bargaining power of suppliersSuppliers of raw materials, components, labor and services are the "market of inputs". Power is high when the supplier or brand is powerful (Apple, Microsoft, auto makers) and has few substitutes. Power also depends on how much a firm buys. The stronger party can demand better prices or terms and raise its margin.Few suppliers, strong brands, few substitute inputs
3. Bargaining power of customers (buyers)The reverse of supplier power: the "market of outputs". Customers can put the firm under pressure on price, quality and terms, and are sensitive to price changes. Examples: Walmart, government agencies.Few large buyers; low switching costs; many alternatives
4. Threat of substitutesA substitute uses a different technology to meet the same need (Kindle for Nook; tea or juice for Starbucks coffee). More substitutes push prices and profit margins down. Firms fight back with switching costs.Many substitutes, low switching costs
5. Rivalry among existing competitorsThe main determinant of industry competitiveness, so it is placed in the centre of the model. Fierce rivalry means expensive advertising, promotions and R&D that cut margins.Entry barriers low, substitutes high, suppliers and buyers try to control the market
  • The intensity of the five forces determines how profitable the industry is (slide 21 MCQ).
  • Students often confuse rivalry with substitutes. Rival = same product (Starbucks vs another coffee shop). Substitute = different product, same need (Starbucks coffee vs tea, juice, soda).

Answers: 1. Rivalry among existing competitors; it is the major determinant of how competitive the industry is. 2. IT that is hard or expensive to copy creates a barrier to entry. 3. A product using a different technology to meet the same economic need.

3.6 Synergies, core competencies, network strategies

  • Synergies: when outputs of some units are used as inputs of others, or two firms pool markets and expertise, costs fall and profits rise. Example: JP Morgan Chase's bank mergers gave it retail branches in new regions. IS tie the merged units together.
  • (textbook) Core competency: an activity at which a firm is a world-class leader. IS that encourage knowledge sharing across units strengthen it.
  • (textbook) Network-based strategies:
    • Network economics: value rises as more people join, at near-zero extra cost (eBay, Facebook).
    • Virtual company model: uses networks to link people, assets and ideas across firms without traditional boundaries (outsourcing production).
    • Business ecosystems: keystone firms create the platform (Apple iOS, Microsoft); niche firms build on it (app developers).

3.7 Scalability

  • Scalability = the ability of a system or business to grow to meet demand; supply can be expanded easily.
  • A scalable company keeps or improves profit margins as sales grow.
  • Test: can you add many more customers without costs rising in proportion? Then it is scalable. Low marginal cost is the key.
  • Examples: software, e-commerce, replicated products (online stores), social media, downloads (apps, music, games), line production and franchising (McDonald's standard Big Mac).
